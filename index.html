UT Bot Alerts

About 40 lines of Pine. One trailing stop.

It is one of the most-liked open-source scripts on TradingView. The code keeps a stop a set number of ATRs from the close and labels the bar where price crosses it. The "EMA crossover" in it is an EMA of length 1, which is the price.

Our rebuild draws the same stop and the same flip bars at the original's settings (checked on 5,000 bars), then adds what the original leaves out.

Open-source scripts

Honest tools, and where they fail.

Each tool makes a familiar chart question easier to inspect. None of them decides for you: markers are neutral and alerts are yours to set.

  1. WaveTrend Audit

    Oscillator panel

    LazyBear's WaveTrend oscillator, rebuilt from scratch with credit. Crosses are marked only once the bar has closed, and a panel counts how often the line sits beyond each level, how often the two lines cross and how many bars pass between crosses, on your own chart. The maths is explained on the WaveTrend page.

    Where it fails: The levels are fixed numbers, not overbought or oversold in any fixed sense, crosses are frequent and mostly near zero, and the counts describe past history only.

    Open on TradingViewRead the teardown

  2. ATR Trailing Stop with Flip Audit

    Trend overlay

    An ATR trailing stop that draws its stop line, confirms flips only at the bar close, and counts how many flips were undone within a few bars, so the cost of a tight setting is visible on your own chart. It rebuilds the stop rule behind UT Bot Alerts from scratch, with credit to the original authors. The method is explained on the UT Bot Alerts page.

    Where it fails: Every flip is late by about the stop distance, and it has no neutral state: it is always on one side, trending or not.

    Open on TradingViewRead the teardown

  3. Sample Size Panel

    Statistics panel

    Enter how many trades you have and the rate that worked, and the panel shows the range the true rate plausibly sits in (a Wilson interval), whether that range can be told apart from 50 percent at all, and roughly how many trades it would take to narrow it, next to how many this chart's history could even give.

    Where it fails: It assumes every trade is an independent draw from one unchanging rate; real trades cluster and regimes change, so the true uncertainty is wider than the panel shows.

    Open on TradingView

  4. Support & Resistance Touch Count

    Levels overlay

    Draws support and resistance zones from recent swing pivots and counts how often price actually returns to them, then compares that rate with a control set of plain round-number levels on the same chart, so you can see whether the zones earn their place.

    Where it fails: An entry is not a reaction: the count says price came back to a zone, not that it turned there, and one chart is one sample.

    Open on TradingView

  5. Swing Ratio Grid

    Levels overlay

    A retracement grid that shows the two things most Fibonacci rules leave out: which swing was measured and which ratio set was used. Includes an arbitrary 0.4 / 0.5 / 0.6 control grid so you can see how little the exact ratios matter.

    Where it fails: The swing depends entirely on the lookback, and a new pivot redraws every level.

    Open on TradingView

  6. Session Range & Value Area Map

    Levels overlay

    Marks where the previous session actually traded: its value area, point of control, and the current session's opening range.

    Where it fails: Prior structure is a fact about the past, not a prediction. Intraday charts only.

    Open on TradingView

  7. Cost Hurdle

    Cost tool

    Shows how much a strategy must earn per trade before fees and slippage are covered.

    Where it fails: The hurdle is arithmetic, not an opinion about your edge.

    Open on TradingView

  8. Regime Tercile Dashboard

    Regime tool

    Splits history into three volatility regimes so you can see which one your results came from.

    Where it fails: Terciles are a crude cut. A regime boundary is a choice, not a discovery.

    Open on TradingView

  9. Backtest Integrity Scanner

    Diagnostic

    Flags the structural problems that make a backtest read better than it should.

    Where it fails: It catches structure, not judgement. A clean scan is not a working strategy.

    Open on TradingView

  10. Volatility Scanner (ATR%)

    Watchlist tool

    Ranks a watchlist by daily range so you know where the movement is.

    Where it fails: volatility isn't direction.

    Open on TradingView

  11. Configurable Stochastics

    Oscillator

    A classic stochastic with selectable SMA, EMA, WMA, or HMA smoothing.

    Where it fails: whipsaws in strong trends.

    Open on TradingView

    From No. 1 (RSI)
    What it actually computes

    Wilder's Relative Strength Index, in full:
    RS  = (smoothed average gain over N) / (smoothed average loss over N)
RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS)
    Two details matter more than the formula itself.

    The "average" is not an average. Wilder used his own smoothing - an exponential filter with alpha = 1/N, not a simple mean of the last N bars.

    The inputs are close-to-close changes. Nothing else enters. Not the high, not the low, not the volume, not the order of events inside the bar.

    So RSI is a smoothed ratio of recent up-closes to recent down-closes, squashed onto a 0-100 scale. That is the entire content. Everything an RSI can tell you is already in the sequence of closes; the indicator is a compression of that sequence, not an addition to it.

    "Overbought" means the security has been going up

    Strip the vocabulary away. A high RSI is a statement that recent closes have risen more than they have fallen, smoothed. That is it. There is no mechanism inside the formula that makes a high reading imply a coming reversal, and nothing in the arithmetic that knows what "too far" means.

    If someone tells you RSI over 70 means a security is due to fall, ask what in the formula produces the word "due". There isn't anything. The claim may still be true for some instrument in some regime - but it is not a property of the indicator, and it has to be demonstrated separately every time.
