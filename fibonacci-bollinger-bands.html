Explainers / Fibonacci Bollinger Bands by Rashad

Fibonacci Bollinger Bands by Rashad, explained from the source

Fibonacci Bollinger Bands (shown on charts as FBB) is an open-source TradingView indicator by Rashad, published in June 2016, with about 33,100 boosts. It draws a centre line and six bands above and six below it, spaced at Fibonacci ratios. This page walks through the published code, says what each part really does, and ends with where it fails. It does not say whether the indicator makes money, and it gives no trades.

Step 1: the centre line

The centre is a 200-bar volume-weighted moving average (VWMA) of the typical price, (high + low + close) / 3. It is not the simple average a normal Bollinger Band uses. Bars with more volume count for more.

The three inputs are the length (200), the price (typical price) and a multiplier (3.0).

Step 2: the width

The width is 3 times the standard deviation of the typical price over the same 200 bars. In Pine that standard deviation is measured around the simple average, not around the volume-weighted centre the bands hang from. When volume is spread evenly the two centres are close; when big moves carry big volume they drift apart.

Step 3: the twelve bands

Each band sits at a fixed fraction of that width: 0.236, 0.382, 0.5, 0.618, 0.764 and 1, above and below the centre. With the default multiplier of 3, that puts the bands at these distances from the centre, in standard deviations:

So every band is an ordinary Bollinger Band with a different multiplier. The 0.618 band is the same line as a 1.854 standard-deviation band on a volume-weighted centre. The Fibonacci numbers decide where the lines are drawn; nothing in the code measures price against a Fibonacci level from a swing high or low, the way a Fibonacci retracement does. 0.764 is 1 minus 0.236, not a ratio from the Fibonacci sequence itself.

Step 4: what the code does not do

There are no alerts, no signals and no other timeframe. Every value comes from the current bar and the 199 before it, so the bands do not repaint: once a bar closes, its values stay. On the live bar they move with each tick until the bar closes, as any band does. The upper and lower bands share the same names in the data window (two lines called "0.236", two called "0.382" and so on), so the data window does not say which is which.

What we checked in Python

We ported the script to Python and ran it on invented random-walk prices with a typical move of 1% a bar, volume that rises with the size of the move, and the default settings: 20 runs of 5,000 bars each.

Prices that wander sit outside every band two to four times as often as the textbook bell curve says. A 200-bar window is long, and a wandering price spends long stretches far from its own 200-bar average. Reading a touch of the 0.618 band as rare is wrong on this kind of data: price was beyond it on 1 bar in 5.

The outer band. The high or low reached one of the two outer bands on about 2.3% of bars.

The two centres. The volume-weighted centre sat a median 0.03 standard deviations from the simple average the width is built on, and at most 0.2. On this data the mismatch is small; it grows on markets where volume clusters on big moves.

These figures describe the code on invented data, not any market.

Where it fails

  • The ratios are spacing, not levels. Each band is a Bollinger Band at a fixed multiple of the standard deviation. Changing the multiplier moves every band together; the Fibonacci names do not add a separate reason for price to react there.
  • Bands are touched more often than they look. On wandering prices, the typical price sat beyond the 0.618 band on about 1 bar in 5 and beyond the inner band on about 2 bars in 3.
  • No volume, no bands. The centre divides by volume. On a symbol with no volume data, the centre and every band have nothing to show. We read this from the code; we did not test it on a live symbol.
  • Two different centres. The bands hang from a volume-weighted average but are sized by a deviation measured around the simple average.
  • 200 bars before anything shows. The first 199 bars have no lines, and every line lags.
  • The live bar moves. Until the bar closes, the bands and the price's position against them can change.
  • The data window names repeat. Upper and lower bands share names, so read the colour or the position, not the label.
  • No alerts. The code has no alert conditions.
